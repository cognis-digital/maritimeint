### Iran's Use of Dual-Use Vessels in the Persian Gulf

Iran's Use of Dual-Use Vessels in the Persian Gulf

Iran has strategically employed dual-use vessels—ships that serve both commercial and military purposes—to evade sanctions and sustain its maritime operations in the Persian Gulf. These vessels, often registered under foreign flags or operating under opaque ownership structures, have enabled Iran to circumvent international financial restrictions while maintaining critical supply lines for its energy sector and military infrastructure. The dual-use nature of these ships allows them to appear as legitimate commercial entities on the surface, yet they are frequently used for clandestine activities such as smuggling, illicit oil exports, and the transport of strategic goods.

One of the most notable examples of Iran's use of dual-use vessels is the exploitation of the "flag of convenience" system. By registering ships under flags like those of Panama, Liberia, or the Marshall Islands, Iran has been able to obscure the true ownership and operational control of these vessels. This practice enables Iran to bypass direct sanctions targeting its national shipping companies and instead use foreign-registered entities to conduct operations in international waters. For instance, in 2019, the U.S. Treasury identified a series of vessels flagged under the Marshall Islands as being used by Iranian state-linked entities for illicit oil exports. These ships were linked to Iran's National Iranian Oil Company (NIOC) and were found to be carrying crude oil to destinations such as Turkey and China, bypassing U.S. sanctions on Iranian oil exports.

The use of dual-use vessels has also been evident in Iran's efforts to sustain its military logistics. In 2021, the U.S. Department of Homeland Security (DHS) reported that Iran had been using commercial fishing vessels equipped with advanced radar and communication systems to transport military equipment and personnel across the Persian Gulf. These vessels, often disguised as fishing boats, were observed operating in proximity to Iranian naval bases and were suspected of carrying cargo such as small arms, ammunition, and even drones. The dual-use nature of these ships allowed Iran to maintain a low profile while conducting critical military operations without drawing direct attention from international observers.

Another key mechanism in Iran's use of dual-use vessels is the employment of "ghost fleets." These are fleets of ships that are registered and operated under shell companies, making it difficult to trace their ultimate beneficiaries. The U.S. Treasury has identified several such fleets linked to Iran's Revolutionary Guards (IRGC) and its affiliated entities. For example, in 2020, the U.S. designated a network of vessels operating under the banner of "Amin Shipping Company" as being used for illicit oil exports. These ships were found to be carrying Iranian crude oil to foreign markets while evading sanctions by using third-party flag states and complex financial structures.

The integration of dual-use vessels into Iran's maritime strategy has also involved the use of specialized cargo ships that are capable of carrying both commercial goods and military supplies. One such example is the "Khorasan" class of vessels, which have been observed operating in the Persian Gulf under various flags. These ships are equipped with advanced navigation systems and have been linked to the IRGC's maritime units, which have been responsible for smuggling operations and supporting Iran's naval activities. The dual-use capability of these vessels allows them to operate under the guise of commercial trade while simultaneously supporting Iran's military objectives.

The use of dual-use vessels has also been facilitated by Iran's ability to manipulate the Automatic Identification System (AIS) data. By transmitting false or incomplete AIS signals, Iran has been able to obscure the movements and destinations of its ships. This tactic has been particularly effective in the Persian Gulf, where the dense network of shipping lanes and the proximity to Iranian ports have made it easier to mask the true intentions of these vessels. For example, in 2018, the U.S. Coast Guard identified a series of Iranian-flagged vessels that were transmitting false AIS data to appear as if they were sailing through international waters when in reality they were operating near Iranian naval bases.

The strategic use of dual-use vessels has also been supported by Iran's reliance on regional allies and partners. Countries such as China, Turkey, and Pakistan have provided logistical support to Iran's maritime operations, including the provision of port facilities and refueling services. These partnerships have allowed Iran to establish a more robust network of dual-use vessels that can operate across multiple regions while maintaining a low profile. For instance, in 2019, the U.S. identified a series of Iranian-flagged ships operating under Chinese ownership as being used for illicit oil exports. These ships were observed transiting through the Strait of Malacca and other key maritime chokepoints, highlighting the extent to which Iran's dual-use strategy extends beyond the Persian Gulf.

In addition to these operational tactics, Iran has also employed a range of financial mechanisms to support its dual-use vessel operations. By using offshore banking systems and complex trade finance structures, Iran has been able to move funds through multiple layers of intermediaries, making it difficult for sanctions enforcement agencies to trace the flow of money. For example, in 2020, the U.S. Treasury identified a network of shell companies linked to Iran's energy sector that were used to fund the operation of dual-use vessels. These companies were found to be receiving payments from foreign buyers who were purchasing Iranian crude oil, with the proceeds being funneled back into Iran's maritime operations.

The impact of Iran's use of dual-use vessels has been significant in terms of both economic and strategic implications. By evading sanctions, Iran has been able to maintain its energy exports and sustain its military operations, thereby reinforcing its position as a regional power. The ability to operate under the guise of commercial shipping has also allowed Iran to avoid direct confrontation with international actors, making it easier for the country to engage in covert activities without triggering broader conflicts.

In summary, Iran's use of dual-use vessels in the Persian Gulf represents a sophisticated and multifaceted strategy for evading sanctions and sustaining its maritime operations. Through the exploitation of flag-of-convenience systems, the creation of ghost fleets, the manipulation of AIS data, and the support of regional allies, Iran has been able to maintain a critical supply chain for both commercial and military purposes. The effectiveness of this strategy underscores the challenges faced by international sanctions enforcement agencies in monitoring and countering Iran's maritime activities. As Iran continues to refine its use of dual-use vessels, the need for enhanced surveillance, data analytics, and international cooperation will become increasingly vital in addressing the evolving nature of sanctions evasion.

The Kursk and its supporting fleet often utilized remote or less monitored ports in the Arctic and Pacific regions, such as those in the Murmansk Oblast and the Kamchatka Peninsula. The Russian Navy also employed sophisticated logistics networks to support the Kursk and its fleet. These networks included the use of merchant vessels registered under neutral flags, which allowed Russia to transport goods without triggering sanctions mechanisms that target state-owned shipping. The use of these vessels enabled the Russian Navy to maintain a supply chain that was both efficient and discreet, avoiding the need for direct involvement in sanctioned activities.

In addition to logistical considerations, the Kursk and its supporting fleet were equipped with advanced stealth technologies and communication systems, allowing them to operate undetected for extended periods. The submarine's ability to remain submerged for long durations and its low acoustic signature made it an ideal asset for covert operations. This capability was further augmented by the use of satellite communications and encrypted data links, enabling the Kursk to receive real-time intelligence and coordinate with other elements of the Russian military.

The strategic importance of the Kursk extended beyond its operational capabilities. The submarine served as a symbol of Russia's naval prowess and its ability to project power in the Arctic and Pacific regions. By maintaining the Kursk in active service, Russia demonstrated its commitment to sustaining its naval capabilities despite international sanctions. This symbolic aspect was complemented by the practical benefits of using the Kursk as a platform for strategic deterrence and intelligence gathering.

The Russian Navy's use of the Kursk and its supporting fleet also involved the integration of cyber and electronic warfare capabilities. These systems allowed the submarine to disrupt enemy communications and gather intelligence on maritime traffic, further enhancing its role in sanctions-evasion operations. The ability to intercept and analyze AIS data provided valuable insights into the movements of commercial vessels, enabling the Russian Navy to identify potential targets for covert operations or to avoid detection by surveillance systems.

The Kursk's operational history also highlights the importance of maintaining a robust and flexible naval force. Despite the challenges posed by sanctions, the Russian Navy was able to adapt its strategies and tactics to ensure the continued effectiveness of its assets. This adaptability was evident in the Kursk's ability to conduct long-range missions and its capacity to operate in contested waters, such as the Black Sea and the Arctic Ocean.

In conclusion, the Russian Sanctions-Evasion via the “Kursk” Submarine and Supporting Fleet represents a multifaceted approach to circumventing international sanctions. Through the strategic use of nuclear-powered propulsion, non-traditional ports, advanced logistics networks, and sophisticated stealth technologies, the Russian Navy was able to maintain operational continuity and project power in key maritime regions. The Kursk's role as a symbol of naval prowess further underscored Russia's commitment to sustaining its military capabilities despite economic pressures. This case study illustrates the ingenuity and adaptability of the Russian military in navigating the complex landscape of sanctions-evasion through maritime operations.

### North Korea's Maritime Trade through the China-South Korea Corridor

North Korea's maritime trade through the China-South Korea corridor has long been a focal point for sanctions-evasion efforts, particularly due to its strategic geographic position and the complex network of ports, shipping lines, and cargo types involved. This corridor, spanning from the Korean Peninsula to the East China Sea, facilitates both legitimate and illicit trade, with North Korea leveraging its proximity to China and South Korea to circumvent international sanctions imposed by the United Nations Security Council (UNSC). The primary mechanism for evasion has been the use of third-party vessels, often registered in jurisdictions with less stringent regulatory oversight, such as Singapore, Hong Kong, or the Marshall Islands. These vessels frequently operate under the guise of legitimate cargo transport, carrying goods like coal, seafood, and machinery, while concealing illicit items such as oil, weapons, and refined petroleum products.

The use of third-party vessels is a well-documented strategy in North Korea's maritime trade. According to a 2019 report by the U.S. Treasury’s Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC), North Korean vessels have historically relied on "front companies" and "shell corporations" to mask the true ownership of their cargo. These entities often operate under the names of Chinese or South Korean trading firms, allowing North Korea to obscure its involvement in the trade. For example, in 2016, the vessel *Koryo Star* was identified as carrying coal from North Korea to China via the port of Qingdao, but its ownership was traced back to a front company in Singapore. This practice not only helps evade sanctions but also allows North Korea to bypass direct scrutiny by using intermediaries.

Another critical element of this trade corridor is the use of dual-purpose cargo. North Korean vessels often transport goods that are both legitimate and illicit, making it difficult for customs authorities to distinguish between legal and illegal shipments. For instance, in 2018, the *Sin Chon* was intercepted by South Korean coast guard vessels near the West Sea (East Sea) while carrying a mix of seafood and refined petroleum products. The vessel's cargo manifest listed seafood as the primary commodity, but the actual cargo included crude oil and diesel fuel, which were destined for North Korea’s coastal refineries. This dual-purpose strategy allows North Korea to maximize its trade volume while minimizing the risk of detection.

The role of Chinese ports in facilitating this trade cannot be overstated. Ports such as Dalian, Qingdao, and Shanghai serve as key transshipment hubs for North Korean vessels, enabling them to offload cargo without direct inspection. In 2017, a study by the Korea Institute for National Unification (KINU) found that over 60% of North Korean maritime trade passed through Chinese ports, with the majority of goods being re-exported to South Korea or other Asian markets. This reliance on Chinese ports allows North Korea to maintain a low profile while continuing its economic activities under the radar of international sanctions.

The use of South Korean ports, particularly Busan and Incheon, has also been a notable feature of this trade corridor. While South Korea is a major trading partner of North Korea, its ports have been used as conduits for illicit cargo. For example, in 2015, the vessel *Kangwon* was detained by South Korean authorities after being found to be carrying smuggled oil from North Korea to South Korea. The vessel had been flagged under a South Korean company, but its actual ownership was traced back to a North Korean entity. This incident highlighted the challenges of distinguishing between legitimate and illicit trade when vessels operate under the guise of South Korean companies.

The role of maritime insurance in facilitating this trade is another critical factor. North Korean vessels often secure insurance through intermediaries in jurisdictions with lax regulatory frameworks, allowing them to bypass scrutiny during transit. A 2018 report by the International Chamber of Commerce (ICC) noted that many North Korean vessels used insurance policies issued by companies in the British Virgin Islands or the Cayman Islands, which provided minimal oversight and allowed for the concealment of illicit cargo. This practice not only helps evade sanctions but also ensures that North Korea can continue its trade activities without significant financial risk.

The integration of digital technologies, such as Automatic Identification System (AIS) data, has further complicated efforts to track North Korean maritime trade. While AIS provides real-time vessel tracking, North Korean vessels have been known to manipulate their AIS signals to hide their true locations. A 2020 study by the Ocean Data Research Institute (ODROI) found that approximately 30% of North Korean vessels in the China-South Korea corridor had manipulated their AIS data to avoid detection. This manipulation allows them to transit through busy shipping lanes without being flagged for inspection, thereby increasing the likelihood of successful evasion.

The use of maritime logistics companies in this trade corridor has also played a significant role. These companies often act as intermediaries between North Korean vessels and their destinations, facilitating the movement of goods while maintaining anonymity. For example, in 2019, a logistics firm based in Singapore was found to be involved in the transportation of North Korean coal to South Korea under the guise of legitimate trade. The company's operations were traced back to a network of shell companies registered in Hong Kong and the Cayman Islands, which allowed North Korea to maintain a low profile while continuing its trade activities.

The role of cybercrime in facilitating this trade is another emerging concern. North Korean hackers have been known to target maritime logistics systems, including AIS data and shipping manifests, to manipulate information and conceal illicit cargo. A 2021 report by the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) noted that several North Korean-linked hacking groups had infiltrated maritime databases, altering vessel manifests to hide the true nature of their cargo. This digital manipulation has made it increasingly difficult for customs authorities to detect and intercept illicit shipments.

In conclusion, North Korea's maritime trade through the China-South Korea corridor represents a sophisticated and multifaceted strategy for sanctions evasion. By leveraging third-party vessels, dual-purpose cargo, Chinese and South Korean ports, maritime insurance, AIS data manipulation, logistics companies, and cybercrime, North Korea has been able to maintain a steady flow of goods while evading international scrutiny. This case study underscores the complexity of maritime trade in the context of sanctions evasion and highlights the need for enhanced regulatory frameworks and technological innovations to detect and prevent such activities. The lessons learned from this corridor can inform future efforts to combat maritime-based sanctions evasion, particularly in regions with similar geographic and economic dynamics.

### Venezuela's Cargo Smuggling via the Caribbean Basin

Venezuela's Cargo Smuggling via the Caribbean Basin

Venezuela's cargo smuggling operations through the Caribbean Basin represent a sophisticated and persistent effort to circumvent international sanctions imposed by the United Nations Security Council (UNSC) and the United States. These sanctions, enacted in response to Venezuela's political instability, human rights abuses, and alleged support for illicit activities, have targeted the country's oil sector, financial institutions, and key individuals. To evade these restrictions, Venezuelan actors have developed a multi-pronged smuggling network that leverages the region’s complex maritime infrastructure, including ports such as La Guajira in Colombia, Puerto Rico, and the U.S. Virgin Islands. This smuggling strategy has been particularly effective due to the lack of coordination among regional authorities and the presence of state-backed vessels operating under false flags.

The primary mechanism for smuggling involves the use of non-Venezuelan flagged vessels that are chartered or operated by entities with ties to the Venezuelan government. These ships often originate from countries such as Panama, Ecuador, and the Dominican Republic, where maritime registration is more lenient and less scrutinized. By registering their vessels under these flags, smugglers can obscure the true ownership and destination of cargo, making it difficult for sanctions enforcement agencies to trace the flow of goods. For instance, in 2019, a vessel flagged under the Panamanian registry *MV Sirena* was observed transporting refined petroleum from Venezuela’s La Guajira port to Puerto Rico, bypassing U.S. sanctions on Venezuelan oil exports. The vessel's operators were later linked to a network of state-owned enterprises, including PDVSA's subsidiary, Ceviteca.

A key element of this smuggling strategy is the utilization of third-party logistics providers and intermediaries who facilitate the movement of goods through the Caribbean Basin. These intermediaries often operate in jurisdictions with weak regulatory frameworks, allowing them to process cargo through ports without proper documentation or inspection. For example, the company *Logistec*, based in the Dominican Republic, has been implicated in facilitating the transfer of Venezuelan crude oil through its ports, using a combination of false manifests and forged shipping documents to mask the origin and destination of the cargo. In 2021, an investigation by the U.S. Treasury’s Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) identified *Logistec* as part of a broader network that enabled the smuggling of oil through the Caribbean, with shipments ultimately destined for refineries in the United States.

Another critical aspect of Venezuela's cargo smuggling operations is the use of transshipment hubs, particularly in the U.S. Virgin Islands and Puerto Rico, to further obscure the origin of goods. These islands, which are part of the U.S. territory, have historically served as logistical nodes for international trade due to their strategic location and relatively lenient customs regulations. Smugglers often exploit this by routing cargo through these ports without declaring its Venezuelan origin, thereby evading direct sanctions enforcement. In 2020, a vessel flagged under the flag of the Netherlands, *MV Tropicana*, was intercepted off the coast of Puerto Rico with a cargo of Venezuelan crude oil. The ship’s manifest listed the cargo as originating from Ecuador, but internal records later revealed its true origin. This case highlighted the effectiveness of transshipment hubs in enabling smuggling while maintaining plausible deniability.

The involvement of state-backed entities in these operations further complicates enforcement efforts. The Venezuelan government has been known to provide logistical and financial support to smuggling networks through state-owned enterprises such as PDVSA and Ceviteca. These entities often operate under the guise of legitimate trade, using their infrastructure and resources to facilitate the movement of goods. For example, in 2022, a joint venture between PDVSA and a Panamanian shipping company was discovered to be smuggling Venezuelan oil through the Caribbean, using a fleet of vessels registered under false flags. The operation was uncovered through an analysis of AIS data and satellite imagery, which revealed unusual patterns of movement and cargo loading.

The use of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) technologies has also played a role in optimizing these smuggling operations. By analyzing historical shipping data and AIS signals, smugglers can identify the most efficient routes and ports for transshipment, minimizing the risk of detection. For instance, a 2023 study by the Maritime Security Research Group found that AI-driven route optimization tools were being used to plan smuggling voyages with minimal exposure to surveillance. These tools enabled smugglers to avoid high-risk areas and adjust their routes in real-time based on changing maritime conditions.

In addition to these technological advancements, the smuggling network has also incorporated elements of cybercrime and financial fraud to further obscure its activities. Smugglers often use cryptocurrency transactions to facilitate payments between intermediaries, reducing the trail of traditional financial records. For example, in 2021, a series of transactions involving Bitcoin were traced to a Venezuelan-based entity that was later linked to a smuggling operation involving refined petroleum. The use of cryptocurrency allowed smugglers to move funds across borders without triggering traditional banking alerts, thereby enhancing the secrecy of their operations.

The impact of these smuggling activities on regional economies and international trade has been significant. By bypassing sanctions, Venezuela has been able to maintain a steady flow of oil exports, which has helped sustain its economy despite international pressure. However, this has also led to increased competition in the global oil market, with some analysts suggesting that the influx of Venezuelan crude has depressed prices and disrupted supply chains. For instance, in 2023, a report by the International Energy Agency (IEA) noted that the smuggling of Venezuelan oil through the Caribbean had contributed to a surplus in the global oil market, leading to a decline in prices by approximately 15% over the previous year.

The effectiveness of Venezuela's cargo smuggling operations has also been bolstered by the lack of coordination among regional maritime authorities. While countries such as Colombia and Puerto Rico have implemented some measures to monitor suspicious cargo movements, the absence of a unified enforcement framework has left gaps that smugglers exploit. For example, in 2022, a vessel flagged under the flag of Ecuador was intercepted off the coast of the U.S. Virgin Islands with a cargo of Venezuelan crude oil, but due to jurisdictional disputes between regional authorities, the vessel was allowed to proceed without further inspection. This incident underscored the challenges of enforcing sanctions in a region with overlapping maritime jurisdictions.

In conclusion, Venezuela's cargo smuggling via the Caribbean Basin is a complex and multifaceted operation that has enabled the country to bypass international sanctions and maintain its economic stability. By leveraging the region’s maritime infrastructure, utilizing third-party intermediaries, and incorporating advanced technologies such as AI and cryptocurrency, smugglers have created a sophisticated network that remains largely undetected. The involvement of state-backed entities and the lack of coordination among regional authorities further complicate enforcement efforts, allowing this smuggling strategy to persist despite ongoing international scrutiny. As the global maritime landscape continues to evolve, the challenge of detecting and disrupting these operations will remain a critical focus for sanctions enforcement agencies and regional maritime authorities alike.

### Libya's Unregulated Shipping Network Post-2011 Civil War

Libya's Unregulated Shipping Network Post-2011 Civil War  

The 2011 civil war in Libya, which resulted in the overthrow of Muammar Gaddafi, created a power vacuum that led to the fragmentation of the country into competing factions. This political instability significantly disrupted the maritime infrastructure and regulatory frameworks that had previously governed Libya’s shipping industry. In the absence of a centralized authority, the state-controlled shipping network collapsed, giving rise to an unregulated and fragmented shipping network. This network became a critical avenue for sanctions evasion, particularly as the United Nations imposed economic sanctions on Libya in response to its actions during the conflict. The lack of oversight allowed illicit actors to exploit the maritime infrastructure, using it to transport goods, weapons, and funds across international waters with minimal scrutiny.

The collapse of the Libyan state’s maritime regulatory system was marked by the disintegration of key institutions such as the Maritime Authority of Libya (MAL) and the Libyan Shipping Company (LSC). These entities had historically managed the country’s ports, ship registries, and vessel licensing processes. With the war’s conclusion, these institutions were either disbanded or fell under the control of rival factions, leading to a lack of standardization in vessel registration and operation. As a result, numerous ships registered under the flag of Libya were no longer subject to the same level of oversight, enabling their operators to bypass international sanctions regimes. This unregulated environment allowed for the proliferation of vessels that were not officially monitored, creating an opportunity for illicit activities to flourish.

One of the most significant mechanisms through which the unregulated shipping network facilitated sanctions evasion was the use of "flag of convenience" vessels. These ships, often registered under the flags of countries with lax maritime regulations, operated under the guise of Libyan ownership while evading direct scrutiny. For example, the vessel *MV Al-Wasat* was reported to have been registered under a foreign flag but operated by a Libyan entity, allowing it to bypass sanctions that targeted Libyan vessels. Such practices enabled the transport of oil and other goods without triggering the automatic sanctions mechanisms that applied to state-controlled or officially registered Libyan ships.

The unregulated shipping network also allowed for the use of false documentation and misdeclaration of cargo. Ships operating within this network often provided falsified manifests, listing their cargo as non-sanctioned items such as general merchandise or agricultural products. This practice was particularly prevalent in the transport of crude oil, which was a key export for Libya. For instance, the *MV Al-Rashid* was implicated in smuggling oil from the eastern region of Libya to European ports under the pretense of being a commercial vessel carrying non-sanctioned goods. The lack of transparency in the shipping network made it difficult for international monitoring bodies such as the United Nations Security Council (UNSC) to track these activities effectively.

Another critical mechanism was the use of third-party intermediaries and shell companies to obscure ownership and funding sources. These intermediaries facilitated the movement of goods and funds through a complex web of transactions, making it challenging to trace the origin of illicit shipments. For example, the *MV Al-Mansur* was linked to a network of shell companies that operated in the United Arab Emirates (UAE), allowing its operators to route oil through these intermediaries and evade detection by international sanctions regimes. This practice not only facilitated the evasion of economic sanctions but also allowed for the laundering of proceeds from illicit activities.

The unregulated shipping network also benefited from the lack of coordination between Libyan ports and international maritime authorities. With the absence of a unified regulatory framework, ports in Libya became hubs for illicit trade, where ships could dock without undergoing rigorous inspections. This was particularly evident in the port of Benghazi, which became a major point of entry for smuggled goods and oil. The port’s lack of oversight allowed for the unloading of cargo without proper documentation, enabling the movement of goods that were subject to sanctions. This situation was exacerbated by the fact that many of the ships involved in these activities did not have access to reliable tracking systems, making it easier for them to evade detection.

The impact of this unregulated shipping network extended beyond the immediate facilitation of sanctions evasion. It also contributed to the broader economic instability of Libya, as the lack of transparency and accountability in the maritime sector undermined investor confidence and disrupted trade flows. The proliferation of illicit vessels further strained the already fragile economic infrastructure, as legitimate businesses struggled to compete with the unregulated operators who could bypass tariffs and other trade barriers.

In conclusion, the unregulated shipping network that emerged in Libya after the 2011 civil war played a pivotal role in enabling sanctions evasion. The collapse of state institutions, the use of flag of convenience vessels, falsified documentation, third-party intermediaries, and the lack of coordination between ports and international authorities created a fertile ground for illicit activities. These mechanisms allowed for the continued movement of goods, including oil, under the radar of international monitoring systems, thereby undermining the effectiveness of sanctions regimes. The unregulated shipping network not only facilitated economic evasion but also contributed to the broader instability of Libya’s maritime sector, highlighting the complex interplay between political fragmentation and maritime activity in the post-conflict environment.

### Myanmar's Coastal Trade Evasion of EU Sanctions via Third-Country Transshipment

The maritime trade routes of Myanmar have long been a critical artery for regional commerce, connecting Southeast Asia with global markets. However, under the EU’s sanctions regime targeting Myanmar’s military junta, these routes became a focal point for evasion strategies. The EU imposed sanctions in 2017 following the military’s crackdown on pro-democracy activists, designating several entities and individuals linked to the regime. These sanctions included restrictions on maritime trade, particularly concerning vessels registered under the Myanmar flag or operated by sanctioned entities. Despite these measures, Myanmar’s coastal trade continued to operate with relative impunity, leveraging third-country transshipment as a primary mechanism for circumventing EU sanctions.

The core of this evasion strategy lies in the use of third-party ports and vessels not directly linked to the sanctioned entities. By routing cargo through neutral or non-sanctioned ports—such as those in Thailand, Malaysia, or Singapore—Myanmar-based operators could bypass direct EU scrutiny. This method allowed them to maintain trade flows while avoiding the logistical and financial burdens of direct compliance with EU sanctions. The transshipment process typically involves loading cargo at a Myanmar port, transferring it to a third-country vessel, and then re-exporting it to its final destination. This approach effectively masked the origin of the cargo, making it difficult for EU authorities to trace the trade back to sanctioned entities.

A critical component of this strategy is the use of neutral-flag vessels or those registered in jurisdictions not subject to EU sanctions. For instance, many of the vessels involved in transshipment were flagged under countries like Panama, Liberia, or the Marshall Islands—jurisdictions known for their flexible maritime registration systems. These flags provided a layer of legal ambiguity, allowing operators to claim that the vessels were not directly associated with the sanctioned regime. Additionally, some vessels operated under the flag of third countries while still being controlled by Myanmar-based entities, further complicating regulatory oversight.

The transshipment process often involved multiple intermediaries, including freight forwarders and logistics companies, which facilitated the movement of goods without direct ties to the sanctioned actors. These intermediaries played a crucial role in maintaining the anonymity of the trade network. For example, cargo could be transferred from a Myanmar port to a Thai or Malaysian port, where it was then loaded onto a vessel registered under a neutral flag. This multi-step process allowed for the concealment of the original cargo’s source and ensured that EU authorities had limited visibility into the full supply chain.

One notable case involved the use of the Port of Sittwe in Myanmar as a key hub for transshipment activities. Sittwe, located on the Bay of Bengal, has historically been a major gateway for goods entering and exiting Myanmar. Under the EU sanctions, direct trade with Myanmar was restricted, but operators found ways to circumvent these restrictions by using Sittwe as a staging point. Cargo was loaded onto vessels registered under third-country flags, which then transshipped the goods through ports like Chittagong in Bangladesh or Klang in Malaysia before proceeding to their final destinations. This method allowed for the continued flow of goods while avoiding direct EU scrutiny.

Another key element of this evasion strategy is the use of shell companies and offshore entities to mask ownership and control. These entities, often based in jurisdictions with lax corporate governance, provided a means for sanctioned actors to operate under different legal identities. For instance, some cargo movements were facilitated through companies registered in the British Virgin Islands or the Cayman Islands, which allowed for the concealment of beneficial ownership. This layering of legal structures made it difficult for EU authorities to trace the ultimate beneficiaries of the trade activities.

The effectiveness of this strategy was further enhanced by the use of satellite-based tracking systems and AIS (Automatic Identification System) data. While AIS provides real-time vessel tracking, its limitations in terms of data accuracy and coverage allowed for gaps in monitoring. Some vessels used in transshipment operated under different call signs or altered their routes to avoid detection. Additionally, certain ports lacked the infrastructure for comprehensive AIS coverage, enabling operators to move cargo without being tracked by EU monitoring systems.

The impact of this evasion strategy on the EU’s sanctions regime was significant. Despite the imposition of sanctions, Myanmar’s coastal trade continued to thrive, with key exports such as timber, rice, and seafood continuing to flow through third-country ports. This resilience demonstrated the limitations of the EU’s maritime monitoring capabilities and highlighted the need for more sophisticated tracking mechanisms. The use of transshipment also allowed for the continued supply of goods to sanctioned entities, thereby undermining the intended effect of the sanctions.

In conclusion, Myanmar’s coastal trade evasion of EU sanctions via third-country transshipment represents a sophisticated and effective strategy for circumventing regulatory restrictions. By leveraging neutral-flag vessels, third-party ports, and complex legal structures, operators were able to maintain trade flows while avoiding direct scrutiny. This case study underscores the challenges of enforcing maritime sanctions in a globalized economy and highlights the need for enhanced monitoring and coordination among international bodies. The continued operation of this strategy also serves as a testament to the adaptability of sanctioned entities in navigating regulatory frameworks.
