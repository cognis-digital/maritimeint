## 8. Basics of Sanctions-Evasion and Maritime Compliance

### Historical Context of Sanctions and Maritime Enforcement

The historical context of sanctions and maritime enforcement is deeply rooted in the geopolitical and economic dynamics of the 20th century, with key developments emerging from major international conflicts and subsequent diplomatic efforts. The first significant instance of sanctions tied to maritime enforcement occurred during World War I, when the British Royal Navy implemented a blockade of Germany to cripple its economy and limit its ability to sustain the war effort. This strategy, known as the "blockade" or "naval blockade," was a direct application of maritime power to enforce economic restrictions. The British used their naval superiority to intercept German merchant ships and confiscate goods, effectively cutting off vital supplies such as food and raw materials. This marked one of the earliest examples of using maritime enforcement as a tool of sanctions, though it was more of a wartime measure than a formalized policy.

The post-World War I era saw the formalization of international sanctions through the League of Nations, which sought to prevent future conflicts by imposing economic restrictions on aggressor states. However, the League's effectiveness in enforcing these sanctions was limited, particularly due to the lack of a unified enforcement mechanism. The most notable example during this period was the League’s response to Japan’s invasion of Manchuria in 1931, which led to the imposition of economic sanctions, including restrictions on trade and access to international markets. While these sanctions were intended to curb Japanese expansionism, they were largely symbolic and lacked the enforcement mechanisms necessary to impact Japan significantly. The failure of the League of Nations to enforce sanctions effectively highlighted the need for a more robust and coordinated system, which would later emerge with the creation of the United Nations.

The Cold War era marked a significant evolution in the use of maritime enforcement as part of sanctions regimes. During this period, the United States and its allies frequently imposed economic sanctions on Soviet-aligned states, often targeting their maritime trade networks to limit their access to global markets. One of the most notable examples was the U.S. imposition of sanctions on Cuba following the Cuban Missile Crisis in 1962, which included a naval blockade known as the "quarantine." This blockade aimed to prevent the shipment of Soviet military equipment to Cuba and to pressure the Cuban government into concessions. The operation was conducted under the authority of the U.S. Navy, with vessels patrolling the Caribbean Sea to intercept suspected shipments. While the blockade did not lead to an immediate resolution of the crisis, it demonstrated the strategic use of maritime enforcement in sanction regimes.

The 1970s and 1980s saw further developments in maritime enforcement as part of broader sanctions strategies. The United Nations imposed economic sanctions on South Africa during the apartheid era, which included restrictions on maritime trade to isolate the country economically. These sanctions were enforced through a combination of international cooperation and individual state actions, with vessels from various nations participating in inspections and monitoring activities. Similarly, the U.S. imposed sanctions on Iran during the 1970s, particularly following the Iranian Revolution, which led to the imposition of an embargo on Iranian oil exports. These sanctions were enforced through maritime checkpoints and inspections, with U.S. naval vessels playing a key role in monitoring compliance.

The post-Cold War period brought about a more sophisticated approach to maritime enforcement within sanctions regimes. The United Nations Security Council began imposing targeted sanctions on states such as Iraq following the Gulf War of 1990-1991. These sanctions included restrictions on maritime trade, particularly the export of oil, which was a critical source of revenue for Iraq. The enforcement of these sanctions relied heavily on the cooperation of international shipping companies and port authorities, with inspections conducted by UN officials and participating states. The effectiveness of these measures was evident in the significant reduction of Iraq’s oil exports during the 1990s, although the lack of a unified enforcement mechanism led to some evasion through alternative routes and smuggling.

The 21st century has seen the continued evolution of maritime enforcement as part of sanctions regimes, with the rise of digital technologies and the increasing complexity of global trade networks. The United States and its allies have increasingly relied on the Automatic Identification System (AIS) to monitor vessel movements and detect potential violations of sanctions. This has been particularly evident in the case of Iran, where U.S. sanctions have targeted the country’s maritime trade, leading to the use of AIS data to track suspected vessels engaged in illicit activities. The integration of AIS with other surveillance technologies has enhanced the ability of enforcement agencies to detect and respond to sanctions violations, although challenges such as signal spoofing and data manipulation remain.

In summary, the historical context of sanctions and maritime enforcement reveals a progression from wartime blockades to formalized international sanctions regimes, with each era reflecting the geopolitical priorities and technological capabilities of the time. The evolution of maritime enforcement has been shaped by the need for effective coordination, technological advancements, and the strategic use of naval power to achieve economic and political objectives. As the global landscape continues to shift, the role of maritime enforcement in sanctions regimes will likely remain a critical component of international policy and enforcement strategies.

### Legal Frameworks Governing Maritime Sanctions Compliance

The legal frameworks governing maritime sanctions compliance are multifaceted, encompassing international treaties, national legislation, and regulatory bodies that enforce compliance with economic sanctions. These frameworks aim to prevent the illicit movement of goods, services, and financial transactions across borders, particularly in contexts where nations or entities are subject to embargoes or restrictions. The United Nations (UN), the European Union (EU), and various national governments have established legal mechanisms to monitor and regulate maritime activities, ensuring that vessels comply with sanctions regimes. These frameworks are critical for detecting and preventing sanctions evasion through maritime routes, which often serve as conduits for illicit trade.

At the core of international maritime sanctions compliance is the United Nations Security Council (UNSC). Through its resolutions, the UNSC has imposed a series of economic sanctions targeting specific states, groups, or individuals. These resolutions typically include measures such as asset freezes, trade restrictions, and prohibitions on financial transactions. For example, Resolution 1441 (2002) imposed sanctions on Iraq, requiring member states to monitor its maritime activities and restrict the import of certain goods. Similarly, Resolution 1718 (2006) targeted North Korea, mandating the monitoring of its shipping to prevent the illicit export of weapons and other restricted materials. These resolutions are enforced through mechanisms such as the UN Sanctions Committee, which oversees compliance and may impose additional measures if violations are detected.

The EU has developed a comprehensive legal framework for maritime sanctions compliance through its Common Foreign and Security Policy (CFSP). The EU’s Regulation (EC) No 2252/2004 establishes a system for monitoring the movement of goods and vessels under sanctions. This regulation allows EU member states to impose measures such as the blocking of assets, restrictions on trade, and the designation of entities or individuals subject to sanctions. Additionally, the EU has established the European Union Naval Force (EU NAVFOR) to monitor maritime activities in regions affected by sanctions, such as the Gulf of Aden and the Mediterranean Sea. These efforts are supported by the EU’s Maritime Surveillance and Monitoring Mechanism, which utilizes satellite imagery and vessel tracking data to detect potential violations.

National legislation also plays a crucial role in maritime sanctions compliance. In the United States, the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) grants the President authority to impose economic sanctions on foreign entities or individuals. The U.S. Treasury’s Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) enforces these sanctions through a system of regulations and enforcement actions. OFAC maintains a list of designated entities and individuals, and vessels operating under U.S. jurisdiction must ensure compliance by reporting transactions involving these entities. For instance, in 2019, OFAC sanctioned several Iranian vessels for their involvement in the illicit transport of oil to countries under U.S. sanctions, highlighting the importance of maritime compliance in enforcing national sanctions policies.

The legal frameworks governing maritime sanctions compliance also include specific provisions related to vessel registration and flag states. Under international law, the flag state is responsible for ensuring that vessels registered under its flag comply with sanctions regimes. This responsibility is reinforced by the International Maritime Organization (IMO) and various bilateral agreements between states. For example, the European Union has established the EU Ship Inspection Regulation, which requires ships flying the EU flag to undergo regular inspections to ensure compliance with sanctions measures. These inspections often involve the use of Automatic Identification System (AIS) data to track vessel movements and detect potential violations.

In addition to these legal mechanisms, the enforcement of maritime sanctions compliance relies on international cooperation and information-sharing. The UN Sanctions Committee maintains a database of sanctioned vessels and entities, which is accessible to member states and international organizations. This database is regularly updated with information from various sources, including intelligence agencies, port authorities, and shipping companies. For instance, in 2018, the UN Sanctions Committee designated several vessels associated with the Iranian Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) for their role in sanctions evasion, leading to increased scrutiny of their maritime activities.

The legal frameworks governing maritime sanctions compliance also address the issue of dual-use goods and technology. Under international law, certain goods and technologies are considered sensitive due to their potential use in military or strategic applications. For example, Resolution 2173 (2014) imposed sanctions on Syria, restricting the import of certain goods, including aircraft parts and electronic components. These restrictions are enforced through customs inspections and vessel tracking systems that monitor the movement of such goods. The European Union has also implemented similar measures under its Regulation (EU) No 833/2014, which restricts the import of goods from Syria and requires vessels to provide detailed documentation for inspection.

The legal frameworks governing maritime sanctions compliance are further reinforced by the use of advanced technologies such as Automatic Identification System (AIS) data. AIS is a critical tool for monitoring vessel movements and detecting potential violations of sanctions regimes. The UN Sanctions Committee has mandated the use of AIS data for tracking vessels subject to sanctions, allowing for real-time monitoring of their movements. For example, in 2017, the UN Sanctions Committee used AIS data to identify a vessel suspected of transporting sanctioned goods from Iran to Syria, leading to its designation as a sanctioned entity.

In conclusion, the legal frameworks governing maritime sanctions compliance are essential for preventing the illicit movement of goods and services across borders. These frameworks include international treaties such as UNSC resolutions, EU regulations, and national legislation such as U.S. OFAC measures. The enforcement of these frameworks relies on mechanisms such as vessel inspections, AIS data monitoring, and international cooperation. By integrating these legal mechanisms, states and international organizations can effectively detect and prevent sanctions evasion through maritime routes, ensuring compliance with economic sanctions regimes.

### AIS Data Structure and Its Role in Sanctions Monitoring

AIS data is structured in a standardized format that enables the tracking of maritime vessels across global waters. The Automatic Identification System (AIS) operates on VHF radio frequencies, transmitting vessel-specific information such as position, speed, heading, and navigational status. Each AIS message is composed of a series of data fields, with specific bit positions designated for particular types of information. For example, the Maritime Safety Traffic (MST) messages contain fields for the vessel's MMSI (Maritime Mobile Service Identity), latitude, longitude, timestamp, and course over ground. These messages are broadcast at regular intervals—typically every few minutes—ensuring continuous vessel tracking. The structure of AIS data is defined by the International Maritime Organization (IMO) and the International Telecommunication Union (ITU), which established the technical specifications for AIS operation in 1999.

The core of AIS data lies in its ability to provide real-time, accurate, and standardized information about a vessel's location and movement. This structure allows for seamless integration with global navigation systems and maritime databases, enabling authorities to monitor ship movements with high precision. The data is transmitted in binary format, with each message containing a header that identifies the type of message (e.g., Class A or Class B), followed by the payload containing vessel-specific details. Class A AIS transponders are typically used on larger vessels, such as cargo ships and tankers, while Class B devices are more common on smaller vessels, including yachts and fishing boats. The distinction between these classes is important for sanctions monitoring, as larger vessels are often associated with higher-value cargo and greater potential for illicit activities.

The role of AIS data in sanctions monitoring is multifaceted. First, it provides a continuous stream of vessel movements that can be analyzed to detect anomalies indicative of sanctions evasion. For instance, if a vessel known to have been sanctioned is detected moving through a restricted zone without reporting its position or altering its course unexpectedly, this could signal an attempt to evade sanctions. Additionally, AIS data can be cross-referenced with other sources, such as port records and customs declarations, to verify the authenticity of a vessel's identity and cargo. This cross-validation is crucial for identifying discrepancies that may indicate illicit trade.

The structure of AIS data also facilitates the identification of vessels operating under false identities or using spoofed signals. For example, a vessel might broadcast a different MMSI than its actual one to mislead tracking systems. Such anomalies can be detected by analyzing patterns in the data and comparing them against known vessel profiles. This capability is particularly valuable in monitoring vessels associated with sanctioned entities or those suspected of engaging in illicit activities.

Moreover, the standardized nature of AIS data allows for the development of sophisticated algorithms and machine learning models that can detect subtle deviations from normal behavior. These models can be trained on historical data to identify patterns indicative of sanctions evasion, such as unusual routing, frequent port changes, or inconsistent speed profiles. By leveraging the structured format of AIS data, authorities can enhance their ability to monitor maritime compliance and detect potential violations more effectively.

In addition to its role in anomaly detection, AIS data serves as a critical tool for tracking the movement of sanctioned vessels across international waters. This is particularly important in regions where surveillance is limited or where vessels may attempt to evade detection by operating in remote areas. The continuous transmission of AIS messages ensures that even vessels in these areas are subject to monitoring, providing authorities with a comprehensive view of maritime activity.

The integration of AIS data into global maritime databases further enhances its utility for sanctions monitoring. These databases compile information from various sources, including port authorities, customs agencies, and shipping companies, allowing for cross-referencing and correlation of vessel movements with other relevant data points. This integration is essential for identifying vessels that may be involved in sanctions evasion, as it enables a more holistic understanding of their activities.

In summary, the structure of AIS data plays a pivotal role in sanctions monitoring by providing real-time, accurate, and standardized information about vessel movements. Its ability to detect anomalies, identify false identities, and support cross-referencing with other data sources makes it an indispensable tool for maritime compliance. As the global shipping industry continues to expand, the importance of AIS data in monitoring and enforcing sanctions will only grow, underscoring its significance in the broader context of maritime security and compliance.

### Common Sanctions-Evasion Tactics in Maritime Operations

The maritime sector has long been a critical conduit for global trade, but it has also become a focal point for sanctions-evasion activities. The inherent complexity of maritime operations—spanning multiple jurisdictions, diverse vessel types, and intricate supply chains—creates numerous opportunities for actors to bypass economic sanctions. These tactics are often sophisticated, leveraging technological innovations and legal loopholes to obscure the true ownership, origin, and destination of cargo. One of the most prevalent strategies is the use of third-party vessels to mask the involvement of sanctioned entities. By employing ships registered under jurisdictions with more lenient compliance regimes or by utilizing vessels that appear to belong to neutral parties, actors can obscure the chain of custody and evade detection by sanctions enforcement agencies.

A key mechanism in this tactic involves the use of "flag of convenience" (FoC) vessels. These are ships registered under a country other than the flag state of their actual owner, often to benefit from lower registration fees, favorable tax policies, or more flexible regulatory environments. For instance, the Republic of the Marshall Islands and Panama have become popular FoC jurisdictions due to their streamlined maritime registration processes. By registering a vessel in one of these countries, actors can obscure the true ownership structure, making it difficult for sanctions enforcement agencies to trace the vessel back to its sanctioned operator. A notable case involved a Russian tanker that operated under a Panamanian flag, allowing it to bypass U.S. sanctions on certain Russian entities by concealing its actual owner. This tactic is further compounded by the use of shell companies and opaque legal entities to further obscure ownership, often through offshore financial centers such as the British Virgin Islands or Seychelles.

Another common strategy involves the use of "ghost fleets" or vessels that are not actively used in trade but are registered and operated under the guise of legitimate maritime activities. These fleets are often used to create false impressions of compliance with sanctions regimes. For example, during the 2010s, a number of Iranian vessels were reported to be operating under the flag of the United Arab Emirates (UAE) or other Gulf states, allowing them to bypass U.S. sanctions on Iran’s energy sector. These vessels were often used for cargo transport that appeared to be legitimate but were in fact part of a broader scheme to move goods and funds through the maritime supply chain without triggering sanctions alerts.

In addition to flag manipulation, the use of intermediaries and third-party logistics providers is another widely employed tactic. These intermediaries can act as conduits for sanctioned cargo by facilitating transactions that appear to be conducted under the guise of legitimate trade. For instance, during the 2014 Ukraine crisis, Russian entities were known to use intermediaries based in Cyprus and Malta to move goods through the Black Sea region without triggering sanctions alerts. These intermediaries often operated under the pretense of providing logistical services, such as port handling or cargo storage, thereby obscuring the true nature of the transaction.

The practice of using "dummy" or "front" companies is another common method in sanctions-evasion schemes. These entities are often created to appear as if they are independent operators, but in reality, they serve as vehicles for sanctioned actors to move goods and funds. For example, during the 2010s, a number of Chinese state-owned enterprises were reported to use front companies based in Singapore and Hong Kong to facilitate trade with sanctioned entities in North Korea. These front companies often operated under the guise of independent trading firms, allowing them to bypass sanctions enforcement mechanisms that require direct attribution to sanctioned entities.

The maritime sector also benefits from the use of complex shipping routes and transit corridors to obscure the origin and destination of cargo. For instance, during the 2015–2016 period, certain Iranian vessels were reported to use the Suez Canal and the Red Sea as transit points to move goods to destinations in Asia and Europe, thereby evading detection by Western sanctions regimes. This tactic is further enhanced by the use of multiple port calls and transshipment hubs, which can complicate the tracking of cargo and make it more difficult for enforcement agencies to trace the movement of goods back to their original source.

The integration of digital technologies into maritime operations has also enabled more sophisticated evasion tactics. For example, the use of Automatic Identification System (AIS) data manipulation allows actors to obscure the true location and movements of vessels. By spoofing AIS signals or using false vessel identifiers, actors can create misleading tracking data that appears to indicate a legitimate trade route while concealing the actual movement of sanctioned cargo. A notable case involved a Russian tanker that used AIS spoofing to appear as if it was transiting through the Baltic Sea, when in reality, it was moving cargo to a port in the Middle East. This tactic is often combined with other methods, such as flag manipulation and intermediary use, to create a multi-layered evasion strategy.

The maritime sector’s reliance on third-party services, such as chartering, freight forwarding, and insurance, also provides opportunities for sanctions-evasion activities. These services can be used to obscure the true ownership and purpose of cargo. For example, during the 2010s, certain Russian entities were reported to use freight forwarders based in Dubai and Singapore to move goods through the maritime supply chain without triggering sanctions alerts. These forwarders often operated under the pretense of providing logistical services, thereby masking the true nature of the transaction.

In conclusion, the maritime sector presents a complex landscape for sanctions-evasion activities, with actors employing a variety of tactics to obscure the movement of sanctioned cargo. The use of third-party vessels, flag manipulation, dummy companies, and digital technologies such as AIS spoofing are among the most common strategies. These tactics often involve multiple layers of obfuscation, leveraging legal loopholes and technological innovations to bypass enforcement mechanisms. As the maritime supply chain continues to evolve, so too will the sophistication of these evasion strategies, necessitating continuous improvements in compliance frameworks and monitoring technologies to detect and mitigate such activities.

### Technical Challenges in Detecting Anomalous Vessel Behavior

The detection of anomalous vessel behavior in maritime compliance presents a complex set of technical challenges that stem from the dynamic nature of oceanic operations, the limitations of available data sources, and the sophisticated methods used by entities to evade sanctions. At the core of these challenges is the reliance on Automatic Identification System (AIS) data as the primary means of tracking vessel movements. While AIS provides a seemingly comprehensive record of ship positions, speeds, and courses, it is not without its vulnerabilities. The system’s open architecture allows for spoofing, where malicious actors can transmit false AIS signals to mislead tracking systems. This has been demonstrated in several high-profile cases, such as the alleged use of AIS spoofing by vessels linked to Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) to bypass sanctions on their oil exports. In these instances, ships would appear to be sailing under a different flag or following an altered route, effectively masking their true identity and destination.

Another critical limitation of AIS data is its susceptibility to signal interference and loss. In congested maritime zones, such as the Strait of Hormuz or the Suez Canal, multiple vessels transmit simultaneously, leading to data collisions and incomplete records. Additionally, certain regions—particularly those with limited infrastructure or where vessels operate in remote areas—may experience intermittent signal coverage. This results in gaps in the tracking record, making it difficult to establish a continuous vessel trajectory. For example, during the 2019–2020 period, several vessels suspected of engaging in sanctions evasion were reported to have "jumped" between AIS signals, creating a fragmented data trail that hindered real-time monitoring efforts.

The challenge of distinguishing between normal operational variations and anomalous behavior further complicates detection. Vessels may alter their routes or speeds due to weather conditions, port congestion, or routine maintenance, which can be mistaken for evasion tactics. Advanced algorithms are required to differentiate between these legitimate deviations and deliberate attempts to evade sanctions. For instance, a vessel might appear to divert its course in the Mediterranean Sea, but this could be due to an unexpected storm rather than a coordinated effort to avoid detection. Machine learning models trained on historical data can help identify patterns that deviate from the norm, but they must be continually refined to account for evolving evasion techniques.

The integration of AIS data with other sources of maritime intelligence introduces additional complexities. While AIS provides real-time positional data, it lacks information about a vessel’s cargo, crew composition, or operational status. To build a more comprehensive picture, analysts often rely on satellite imagery, port records, and customs declarations. However, these data sources are not always synchronized or accessible in real time. For example, satellite imagery may reveal a vessel’s presence at a specific location, but without corroborating AIS data, it is difficult to determine its exact movement or intent. Similarly, port records may indicate that a vessel has docked at a sanctioned location, but the absence of AIS data during the docking period could suggest an attempt to conceal its activities.

The issue of data quality also plays a significant role in the detection of anomalous behavior. AIS signals are transmitted in a standardized format, but the accuracy of these signals can vary depending on the vessel’s equipment and the environment. Older or poorly maintained transponders may transmit incorrect position data, leading to inaccuracies in tracking. This has been observed in certain vessels operating under the flag of Venezuela, where inconsistent AIS reporting has raised concerns about the reliability of their tracking data. Furthermore, some vessels may intentionally transmit false positions to mislead monitoring systems, as seen in the case of a Chinese vessel linked to the China Ocean Shipping Company (COSCO) that was reported to have altered its route in the Gulf of Aden to evade sanctions scrutiny.

The dynamic nature of maritime operations also presents technical challenges in detecting anomalies. Vessels may change their speed or course in response to external factors such as fuel efficiency, cargo distribution, or regulatory requirements. These variations can create patterns that mimic evasion tactics, making it difficult to determine whether a deviation is intentional or operational. For example, a vessel might slow down its speed in the Atlantic Ocean to conserve fuel, which could be mistaken for an attempt to avoid detection by reducing its AIS signal visibility. Advanced analytics and real-time monitoring systems must therefore be capable of distinguishing between these legitimate operational changes and deliberate evasion strategies.

The integration of multiple data sources also introduces challenges related to data synchronization and interoperability. While AIS data is widely used, it is often complemented by other maritime intelligence tools such as Automatic Identification System (AIS) spoofing detection systems, satellite tracking, and port monitoring networks. However, these systems may operate on different platforms and protocols, leading to discrepancies in the data they produce. For instance, a vessel may be tracked accurately via AIS but may not appear in satellite imagery due to technical limitations or operational constraints. This lack of synchronization can create gaps in the detection process, allowing vessels to evade scrutiny by exploiting differences in data sources.

Finally, the technical challenges in detecting anomalous vessel behavior are exacerbated by the evolving tactics used by entities seeking to evade sanctions. As monitoring systems become more sophisticated, evasion techniques have also advanced, incorporating elements such as dual AIS transponders, signal jammers, and multi-layered routing strategies. These methods allow vessels to transmit conflicting or false information, making it difficult for analysts to determine the true location or identity of a vessel. For example, a vessel may transmit two different AIS signals simultaneously—one under a sanctioned flag and another under a neutral one—creating confusion in the tracking system. This level of sophistication requires advanced detection algorithms that can analyze multiple data streams and identify inconsistencies that may indicate evasion.

In summary, the technical challenges in detecting anomalous vessel behavior are multifaceted, stemming from the limitations of AIS data, the complexity of distinguishing between normal operations and evasion tactics, and the integration of diverse data sources. These challenges necessitate the development of advanced analytical tools and robust monitoring systems capable of identifying subtle deviations from expected behavior. By addressing these technical hurdles, maritime compliance efforts can more effectively detect and respond to sanctions-evasion activities.

The *Sahel* case demonstrated the vulnerability of sanctions regimes to flag substitution and AIS spoofing, prompting calls for enhanced monitoring of maritime traffic and stricter enforcement of vessel registration requirements.
