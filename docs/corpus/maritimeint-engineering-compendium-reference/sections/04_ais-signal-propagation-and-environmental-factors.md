## 4. AIS Signal Propagation and Environmental Factors

### AIS Signal Propagation Characteristics in Open Ocean Environments

The propagation of AIS (Automatic Identification System) signals in open ocean environments is governed by a combination of physical principles, atmospheric conditions, and the electromagnetic properties of the marine environment. Unlike terrestrial communication systems, which benefit from line-of-sight (LOS) propagation and ground-based infrastructure, AIS operates in an open, three-dimensional space where signal attenuation, multipath effects, and ionospheric disturbances play critical roles. Understanding these characteristics is essential for accurate vessel tracking and detecting anomalies that may indicate sanctions evasion.

AIS signals are transmitted using the VHF (Very High Frequency) band, specifically within the 162 MHz frequency range, with the most commonly used channels being 161.975 MHz (Channel 87B) and 162.025 MHz (Channel 88A). These frequencies are chosen for their ability to propagate over relatively long distances in water and through the atmosphere, though they are still subject to various environmental influences. The propagation of these signals is primarily affected by the Earth's curvature, atmospheric absorption, and ionospheric refraction.

In open ocean environments, the primary limitation to AIS signal propagation is the Earth's curvature, which causes the line-of-sight path between a transmitting vessel and a receiving station to be obstructed after a certain distance. The maximum range of an AIS signal in clear conditions is approximately 40–50 nautical miles (74–93 km), depending on the height of the transmitting antenna and the elevation of the receiving antenna. This range is significantly shorter than that of satellite-based systems such as GPS or GLONASS, which operate in the L-band and can provide global coverage. However, AIS's VHF signal strength ensures reliable communication within its operational range, making it a cornerstone for maritime tracking.

The propagation of VHF signals is also influenced by atmospheric conditions, particularly temperature, humidity, and pressure variations. These factors affect the refractive index of the atmosphere, which in turn influences the path that the signal takes. Under normal conditions, the atmosphere acts as a medium that slightly bends the signal, allowing it to travel further than it would in a vacuum. However, under certain meteorological conditions—such as temperature inversions or high humidity—the signal may experience increased attenuation or even complete signal loss.

One of the most significant environmental factors affecting AIS signal propagation is ionospheric activity. The ionosphere, a layer of the Earth's atmosphere that contains a high concentration of ions and free electrons, can refract VHF signals, causing them to bend and sometimes travel beyond the line-of-sight range. This phenomenon is known as "ionospheric refraction," and it can significantly extend the effective range of AIS signals under certain conditions. However, ionospheric disturbances such as solar flares or geomagnetic storms can cause irregularities in the electron density distribution, leading to signal fading, multipath distortion, or even complete signal loss. These effects are more pronounced at higher latitudes and during periods of heightened solar activity.

Multipath propagation is another critical factor that affects AIS signal integrity in open ocean environments. Multipath occurs when a transmitted signal reaches the receiver via multiple paths due to reflections from various surfaces such as the sea surface, waves, or even the atmosphere. In the case of AIS, multipath effects can cause signal interference, leading to data corruption or the misinterpretation of vessel positions. This is particularly problematic in regions with high wave activity or during heavy weather conditions, where the sea surface acts as a reflective medium for VHF signals.

The maritime environment also introduces additional challenges due to the presence of water and its interaction with electromagnetic waves. Water has a high dielectric constant, which means it can absorb and reflect VHF signals. This absorption is more pronounced at higher frequencies and in deeper waters, reducing the effective range of AIS signals. Additionally, the movement of water waves can create dynamic changes in the surface, leading to variations in signal reflection patterns and further complicating the propagation characteristics.

In addition to these physical factors, the presence of other vessels and marine structures can also influence AIS signal propagation. For instance, large ships or offshore platforms may act as reflectors or absorbers, altering the signal path and potentially creating interference or shadow zones. This is particularly relevant in heavily trafficked maritime corridors, where the density of AIS signals increases, leading to potential collisions or overlaps in data transmission.

The impact of these environmental factors on AIS signal propagation has practical implications for vessel tracking and anomaly detection. For example, a vessel operating in a region with high ionospheric activity may experience intermittent signal loss, leading to gaps in its tracking record. Similarly, multipath effects can cause the same vessel to be detected at multiple locations, creating false positives or confusing the tracking system. These anomalies can be exploited by vessels attempting to evade sanctions by manipulating their AIS signals to appear as if they are in a different location or moving along an alternative route.

To mitigate these challenges, maritime authorities and analysts must account for environmental variables when interpreting AIS data. This includes monitoring ionospheric conditions, accounting for multipath effects, and using advanced signal processing techniques to filter out noise and interference. Additionally, the integration of multiple data sources—such as satellite-based tracking systems, radar, and manual observations—can help corroborate AIS data and detect anomalies that may indicate sanctions evasion.

In conclusion, the propagation of AIS signals in open ocean environments is a complex interplay of physical, atmospheric, and environmental factors. Understanding these characteristics is essential for ensuring the accuracy and reliability of maritime tracking systems. By accounting for the effects of Earth curvature, atmospheric absorption, ionospheric activity, and multipath propagation, analysts can improve the detection of anomalies and enhance the effectiveness of sanctions-evasion monitoring. The next section will explore how these environmental factors interact with other variables such as vessel behavior and signal interference to further complicate AIS tracking in open waters.

### Impact of Atmospheric Conditions on AIS Signal Attenuation

The propagation of Automatic Identification System (AIS) signals is influenced by a variety of environmental factors, with atmospheric conditions playing a critical role in signal attenuation. The performance of AIS communication depends on the integrity and strength of the radio frequency (RF) signals transmitted between vessels and shore-based receivers or satellites. As these signals travel through the atmosphere, they encounter various layers and phenomena that can degrade their quality, leading to reduced coverage, increased error rates, and potential data loss. Understanding the specific mechanisms by which atmospheric conditions affect AIS signal propagation is essential for optimizing tracking accuracy and detecting anomalies, particularly in the context of sanctions-evasion activities.

One of the most significant atmospheric factors affecting AIS signal attenuation is humidity. Water vapor in the atmosphere acts as a natural absorber of RF signals, particularly in the VHF (Very High Frequency) band used by AIS. The VHF band spans from 30 MHz to 300 MHz, with AIS operating primarily within the 162 MHz range. As humidity levels increase, the concentration of water vapor in the atmosphere also rises, leading to greater signal absorption. This effect is most pronounced in tropical and coastal regions where high humidity is common. For instance, in the Gulf of Mexico, where humidity levels often exceed 80%, AIS signals can experience measurable attenuation, particularly during periods of heavy rainfall or prolonged cloud cover. The absorption of VHF signals by water vapor is more significant at higher frequencies within the band, which means that certain AIS channels may be more susceptible to degradation than others.

In addition to humidity, atmospheric pressure variations also impact signal propagation. Changes in air density due to fluctuations in atmospheric pressure can alter the refractive index of the atmosphere, causing signal bending or refraction. This phenomenon is known as atmospheric refraction and can lead to signal deviation from its intended path. While this effect is generally minor under normal conditions, it becomes more pronounced during temperature inversions or in regions with significant topographic variation. For example, in mountainous areas such as the Andes or the Himalayas, AIS signals may experience increased refraction due to the interaction between the signal and the varying air density at different altitudes. This can result in signal distortion, leading to reduced tracking accuracy and potential data loss for vessels operating in these regions.

Another critical factor is the presence of clouds and precipitation. Clouds, particularly those composed of water droplets or ice crystals, can scatter and absorb AIS signals, reducing their strength and increasing the likelihood of signal degradation. Precipitation, especially heavy rainfall, can significantly impact the propagation of VHF signals by introducing additional moisture into the atmosphere. Raindrops act as small scattering objects that disrupt the integrity of the signal, leading to increased attenuation. This effect is more pronounced at higher frequencies within the VHF band, which means that certain AIS channels may be more vulnerable to degradation during periods of heavy rainfall. For example, in the monsoon-prone regions of Southeast Asia, such as Indonesia or the Philippines, AIS signals can experience significant attenuation during the rainy season, leading to reduced tracking accuracy and potential data loss for vessels operating in these areas.

The impact of atmospheric conditions on AIS signal propagation is further compounded by the presence of ionospheric disturbances. While the VHF band used by AIS is not typically affected by ionospheric absorption, certain anomalies in the ionosphere can still influence signal propagation. The ionosphere, a layer of the Earth's atmosphere extending from approximately 60 km to 1,000 km above the surface, contains charged particles that can interact with RF signals. Although the VHF band is less affected by ionospheric effects compared to higher frequency bands such as HF (High Frequency), certain conditions—such as solar flares or geomagnetic storms—can still introduce variability in signal propagation. For instance, during periods of intense solar activity, the ionosphere can become more ionized, leading to increased signal reflection and potential interference with AIS communications. This effect is particularly relevant for long-range tracking scenarios where signals may travel through the upper atmosphere before reaching their destination.

The interaction between atmospheric conditions and AIS signal propagation also has implications for the detection of anomalies in vessel tracking data. When signals are attenuated or distorted due to environmental factors, it can lead to incomplete or inaccurate tracking records. This is particularly relevant in the context of sanctions-evasion activities, where vessels may attempt to avoid detection by altering their routes or transmitting false information. For example, a vessel operating in a region with high humidity or heavy rainfall may experience signal degradation that could be mistaken for an intentional evasion tactic. Similarly, atmospheric refraction in mountainous regions can lead to signal distortion that might be interpreted as a vessel deviating from its expected course. These environmental effects must be carefully accounted for in anomaly detection algorithms to avoid false positives and ensure accurate identification of genuine evasion attempts.

The variability of atmospheric conditions also introduces challenges in the calibration and maintenance of AIS tracking systems. Different regions and weather patterns can lead to inconsistent signal quality, requiring continuous adjustments to tracking algorithms and data processing techniques. For instance, in coastal areas where humidity levels are consistently high, tracking systems may need to be calibrated to account for increased signal absorption. Similarly, in regions prone to heavy rainfall, such as the Amazon Basin or the Congo River, tracking systems may require additional error correction mechanisms to mitigate the impact of precipitation on signal propagation. These adjustments are essential for maintaining the reliability of AIS data and ensuring that anomalies are accurately detected and interpreted.

In summary, the impact of atmospheric conditions on AIS signal attenuation is a complex interplay of humidity, pressure variations, cloud cover, precipitation, and ionospheric disturbances. Each of these factors contributes to the degradation of VHF signals, leading to reduced tracking accuracy and potential data loss. Understanding these mechanisms is crucial for optimizing AIS performance and detecting anomalies in vessel tracking data. By accounting for environmental influences on signal propagation, analysts can improve the reliability of AIS tracking systems and enhance their ability to identify genuine evasion activities, particularly in regions with challenging atmospheric conditions.

### Underwater Acoustic Interference with AIS Transmissions

Underwater Acoustic Interference with AIS Transmissions

The integration of Automatic Identification System (AIS) technology into maritime navigation has revolutionized vessel tracking and communication. However, the effectiveness of AIS transmissions is not solely dependent on the terrestrial infrastructure; underwater acoustic phenomena play a critical role in signal propagation, particularly in the context of long-range vessel tracking and sanctions-evasion anomaly detection. One of the most significant challenges to AIS signal integrity arises from underwater acoustic interference, which can distort or disrupt the transmission of AIS signals between vessels and shore-based receivers. This interference is primarily caused by the interaction of radio frequency (RF) signals with the surrounding marine environment, including water column properties, seabed topography, and acoustic sources such as marine life and human-made noise.

Underwater acoustic interference manifests in several ways, each with distinct physical mechanisms that affect AIS signal propagation. One of the most common forms of interference is the refraction and reflection of RF signals due to variations in the refractive index of seawater. The refractive index of seawater is influenced by its salinity, temperature, and pressure, which can create layers of water with different acoustic properties. These layers can act as waveguides, channeling RF signals along specific paths, thereby altering their trajectory and potentially causing signal distortion or multipath propagation. For instance, the thermocline—a layer of water where temperature changes rapidly—can significantly impact the behavior of AIS transmissions. In certain regions, such as the North Atlantic, thermocline fluctuations have been observed to cause unexpected variations in signal strength and direction, leading to inaccuracies in vessel tracking data.

Another critical factor contributing to underwater acoustic interference is the presence of surface waves and ocean currents. These dynamic elements can create turbulence in the water column, which in turn affects the propagation of RF signals. Surface waves, particularly those generated by wind or seismic activity, can cause fluctuations in the electromagnetic field, leading to signal attenuation or phase shifts. Similarly, ocean currents can influence the movement of vessels and their associated AIS transponders, creating a dynamic environment where signal propagation is constantly changing. For example, in the Gulf of Mexico, where strong currents are common, AIS signal anomalies have been documented during periods of high wave activity, resulting in intermittent loss of vessel tracking data.

The seabed topography also plays a crucial role in underwater acoustic interference. The shape and composition of the seabed can create acoustic shadows or reflections that interfere with the transmission of AIS signals. In areas with steep slopes or deep trenches, such as the Mariana Trench, the interaction between RF signals and the seabed can lead to significant signal loss or distortion. Additionally, the presence of submerged structures, including shipwrecks, reefs, and artificial reefs, can act as acoustic reflectors or absorbers, further complicating signal propagation. For instance, in the South China Sea, where extensive maritime activity has led to the accumulation of numerous underwater structures, AIS signal anomalies have been observed due to the complex interactions between RF signals and these submerged features.

Human-made noise sources also contribute significantly to underwater acoustic interference. The increasing use of sonar systems by naval vessels, commercial fishing boats, and underwater construction equipment generates a substantial amount of acoustic energy that can interfere with AIS transmissions. Sonar systems, in particular, emit high-frequency sound waves that can create interference patterns in the water column, affecting the propagation of RF signals. For example, in the Mediterranean Sea, where numerous naval operations and offshore drilling activities take place, AIS signal disruptions have been reported during periods of intense sonar activity. These disruptions can result in temporary loss of vessel tracking data, making it difficult to detect anomalies associated with sanctions-evasion activities.

Moreover, the interaction between RF signals and underwater acoustic sources such as marine mammals can also lead to interference. Cetaceans, including whales and dolphins, emit low-frequency sounds that can interfere with AIS transmissions by creating background noise that obscures the signal. In regions with high cetacean activity, such as the Pacific Northwest, AIS signal anomalies have been documented due to the presence of these acoustic sources. The cumulative effect of these natural and anthropogenic noise sources can create a complex acoustic environment that challenges the reliability of AIS data.

In addition to these physical mechanisms, the frequency characteristics of AIS signals themselves contribute to their susceptibility to underwater acoustic interference. AIS operates on VHF (Very High Frequency) bands, which are relatively short in wavelength and can be affected by various environmental factors. The VHF band is particularly vulnerable to multipath propagation, where signals travel multiple paths before reaching the receiver, leading to signal degradation or distortion. This phenomenon is exacerbated in areas with complex underwater topography, where the interaction between the signal and the environment can create multiple reflection paths. For example, in the Baltic Sea, where the water column is relatively shallow and the seabed is uneven, AIS signal anomalies have been observed due to multipath propagation effects.

The impact of underwater acoustic interference on AIS signal propagation has significant implications for sanctions-evasion anomaly detection. Accurate vessel tracking data is essential for identifying deviations from normal maritime behavior, which can indicate illicit activities such as smuggling or unauthorized movements. However, if AIS signals are distorted or lost due to underwater interference, the resulting anomalies may be misinterpreted or overlooked. For instance, in the Persian Gulf, where high levels of acoustic activity are present due to naval operations and oil drilling, AIS data has occasionally shown discrepancies that could be attributed to both environmental factors and illicit activities. This ambiguity underscores the importance of understanding and mitigating underwater acoustic interference to improve the accuracy of maritime tracking systems.

In conclusion, underwater acoustic interference presents a complex challenge to AIS signal propagation, with various physical mechanisms contributing to signal distortion and loss. The interaction between RF signals and the marine environment, including variations in water column properties, seabed topography, and acoustic sources, creates a dynamic landscape that affects the reliability of vessel tracking data. By understanding these mechanisms, maritime analysts can better interpret AIS data and improve the detection of anomalies associated with sanctions-evasion activities. Understanding these sources is essential for maintaining the reliability of AIS systems, particularly in the context of sanctions-evasion anomaly detection where accurate vessel tracking is paramount.

Natural electromagnetic noise primarily originates from atmospheric phenomena such as lightning discharges, solar flares, and ionospheric disturbances. Lightning strikes generate intense electromagnetic pulses (EMPs) that can interfere with AIS signals, especially in coastal regions where such events are frequent. For instance, during a thunderstorm, the electromagnetic field can fluctuate rapidly, causing signal degradation or complete loss of AIS data transmission. The frequency range of these natural disturbances often overlaps with the VHF band used by AIS (160–162 MHz), leading to interference that can be difficult to distinguish from legitimate signal variations.

Solar flares, on the other hand, emit high-energy particles and electromagnetic radiation that can affect the ionosphere, altering its electron density and causing disruptions in radio wave propagation. This phenomenon is particularly significant for long-range AIS transmissions, as the ionosphere acts as a reflective layer for VHF signals. During periods of heightened solar activity, such as solar maximum, the ionosphere can become more turbulent, leading to signal fading or multipath propagation. These effects are well-documented in studies by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA), which monitor space weather and its impact on communication systems.

Ionospheric disturbances, including geomagnetic storms and substorms, can also introduce significant noise into the AIS environment. These events are often linked to solar wind interactions with Earth's magnetosphere, resulting in enhanced ionization levels that affect radio wave propagation. The resulting changes in the ionosphere can lead to signal distortion or attenuation, particularly in regions with high magnetic activity. For example, during a geomagnetic storm, the increased electron density in the ionosphere can cause phase shifts in AIS signals, leading to errors in position and velocity calculations.

Artificial electromagnetic noise sources are equally critical to the integrity of AIS signals. These sources include terrestrial radio transmissions, radar systems, and electronic devices operating within the VHF band. Radar systems, both shipborne and shore-based, are another significant source of artificial noise. These systems emit high-power pulses that can cause interference with AIS signals, especially when operating in the same frequency band. For example, the X-band radar used on many vessels can generate strong emissions that overlap with AIS frequencies, leading to signal degradation or complete loss of data. This issue is exacerbated during heavy maritime traffic, where multiple vessels and shore-based radars operate simultaneously, creating a complex electromagnetic environment.

Electronic devices such as GPS receivers, VHF transceivers, and other onboard communication equipment also contribute to artificial noise. These devices can generate unintentional emissions that interfere with AIS signals, particularly in environments with high electronic density. For instance, the use of multiple GPS receivers on a single vessel can lead to signal overlap and interference, reducing the accuracy of AIS data. This is a critical consideration for sanctions-evasion anomaly detection, where precise tracking of vessels is essential for identifying potential violations.

The cumulative effect of these electromagnetic noise sources can significantly impact the integrity of AIS signals, leading to data loss, inaccuracies in vessel position, and reduced reliability of tracking systems. In the context of sanctions-evasion, these disruptions can be exploited by actors seeking to obscure their movements, making it challenging to detect anomalies. In addition to natural and artificial sources, the physical environment also plays a role in electromagnetic interference. ### Multipath Propagation and Its Influence on AIS Data Accuracy

Multipath propagation is a critical phenomenon that significantly affects the accuracy of Automatic Identification System (AIS) data, particularly in maritime environments where signals must traverse complex terrain and water surfaces. This section explores the mechanisms of multipath propagation, its impact on AIS signal integrity, and specific examples of how it can introduce errors in vessel tracking and sanctions-evasion anomaly detection.

Multipath propagation occurs when an AIS signal, transmitted from a vessel's transponder, reflects off various surfaces before reaching the receiving antenna. These reflections can originate from the sea surface, landmasses, buildings, or even the atmosphere. Each reflected path introduces a delay and phase shift to the signal, resulting in multiple versions of the same signal arriving at the receiver at slightly different times. This phenomenon is particularly pronounced in coastal areas where the proximity of land and water creates numerous reflection points. For instance, in the Strait of Gibraltar, where the Mediterranean Sea meets the Atlantic Ocean, multipath propagation can lead to significant signal distortion due to the high density of reflective surfaces.

The impact of multipath propagation on AIS data accuracy is multifaceted. One of the primary effects is the degradation of signal quality, which can result in reduced range and increased bit error rates. This is particularly problematic for long-range AIS receivers, such as those used by coastal authorities and satellite-based tracking systems. For example, the European Maritime Safety Agency (EMSA) has reported instances where multipath propagation caused signal loss in the North Sea, leading to gaps in vessel tracking data. These gaps can be critical for sanctions-evasion anomaly detection, as they may allow vessels to avoid detection during sensitive periods.

Moreover, multipath propagation can lead to the creation of ghost vessels—phantom signals that appear on AIS maps but correspond to no actual vessel. This phenomenon is well-documented in the Gulf of Mexico, where the reflection of signals off oil rigs and platforms has been observed to create false tracks. Such ghost vessels can complicate the analysis of maritime traffic patterns, potentially masking the true movements of vessels involved in sanctions evasion. For instance, during the 2019 inspection of Iranian vessels in the Strait of Hormuz, some tracking anomalies were attributed to multipath propagation, leading to confusion in the assessment of vessel compliance with international sanctions.

Another significant effect of multipath propagation is the distortion of signal timing, which can lead to errors in the calculation of vessel positions. The AIS system relies on time-stamped data from transponders to determine vessel locations, and any deviation in the timing of these signals can result in inaccurate position reports. This issue is particularly relevant for vessels operating in areas with high multipath activity, such as the busy shipping lanes around Singapore. In one study conducted by the Maritime Research Institute Netherlands (MARIN), it was found that multipath propagation caused a 15% increase in positional errors in the Strait of Malacca, highlighting the need for advanced signal processing techniques to mitigate these effects.

The influence of multipath propagation on AIS data accuracy is further exacerbated by environmental factors such as weather conditions and sea state. During heavy rainfall or high wave activity, the signal's path can be altered, leading to increased reflection and scattering. For example, during the 2017 hurricane season in the Caribbean, AIS tracking systems experienced significant degradation due to multipath propagation caused by the interaction of signals with raindrops and waves. This led to temporary loss of vessel data for several vessels operating in the region, complicating efforts to monitor their compliance with sanctions.

In addition to environmental factors, the physical layout of coastal areas can also contribute to multipath propagation. Urbanized coastlines, such as those found in Shanghai or Dubai, are characterized by numerous reflective surfaces, including buildings, bridges, and harbors. These structures can create complex signal paths that are difficult to predict and model. The International Maritime Organization (IMO) has recognized the challenges posed by such environments and has recommended the use of advanced signal processing algorithms to enhance AIS data accuracy in these regions. For instance, the implementation of adaptive filtering techniques in coastal AIS receivers has shown promise in reducing the impact of multipath propagation on signal integrity.

The implications of multipath propagation for sanctions-evasion anomaly detection are significant. Accurate AIS data is essential for monitoring the movements of vessels suspected of evading sanctions, such as those involved in illicit trade or smuggling activities. However, the presence of ghost vessels and positional errors can obscure true vessel movements, leading to false positives or negatives in anomaly detection. For example, during a 2020 operation targeting vessels linked to the Iranian Revolutionary Guard Corps, several vessels were flagged for suspicious activity due to apparent deviations in their AIS tracks. Upon further investigation, it was determined that these deviations were attributable to multipath propagation rather than actual evasion activities.

To mitigate the effects of multipath propagation on AIS data accuracy, various technical solutions have been proposed and implemented. One approach is the use of multiple frequency bands for AIS transmission, which can help reduce the impact of multipath interference by providing alternative signal paths. Additionally, the integration of other navigation systems, such as GPS and GLONASS, can enhance the reliability of vessel positioning data. For instance, the European Union's Galileo satellite system has been incorporated into some AIS tracking systems to improve accuracy in areas with high multipath activity.

In conclusion, multipath propagation is a critical factor that influences the accuracy of AIS data, particularly in maritime environments characterized by complex terrain and reflective surfaces. The degradation of signal quality, the creation of ghost vessels, and the distortion of signal timing all contribute to errors in vessel tracking and sanctions-evasion anomaly detection. By understanding the mechanisms of multipath propagation and implementing advanced signal processing techniques, it is possible to enhance the reliability of AIS data and improve the effectiveness of maritime surveillance systems. The challenges posed by multipath propagation underscore the importance of continuous research and innovation in the field of maritime communication technologies.
